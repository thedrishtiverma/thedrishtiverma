
# Drishti Verma

> **I build software by paying unusual attention to people.**

## 00 · Story

I was a creative kid before I was anything else.

Drawing mostly. I'd see something in my head and want to get it out — first on paper, then into models, science projects, anything I could make with my hands. The skill I was actually building without knowing it wasn't drawing.

It was the loop: observe something, imagine it differently, make it real.

I've been running that loop my whole life. It just looks different now.

Covid happened. Everything moved online.

I was 14, watching the world reorganize itself around screens, and in between something clicked —
> You can build things here that reach millions of people. Not someday. Now.

That thought never really left.

I grew up in India, childhood spent being student (I am still one). And what you notice, when you pay attention, is how many people around you are living someone else's idea of a good life.

Indian society has strong opinions about what success looks like. I felt that pressure myself — the invisible walls around which careers were acceptable, which dreams were practical, which choices were even on the table.

UNTRAP isn't an abstract problem to me. I watched it happen to people I know. I felt versions of it myself.

The thing that shaped me most had nothing to do with software.

I prepared for the National Defence Academy (NDA) examination — for a long time.

I didn't make it through the interview. I was devastated. Genuinely depressed for a while. Withdrew from people, buried myself in distractions just to stop the thoughts, stopped journalling. Then, slowly, I came out of it. I started journalling, again — I've been doing it for six years now.

And what I came out with wasn't self-satisfaction but clarity. I want to be successful in life building my own stuff.

The most important exam isn't the one you just failed. It's the one you take every day with your own life.

I've been building toward that ever since.

## 01 · Projects I'm Building

**[UNTRAP](https://untrap.lovable.app/)** — _for a student, by a student._

I watched engineering students in my college — my circle, my generation — spend years building someone else's future. Not because they were lazy. Because nobody showed them there was a door. UNTRAP is LLM-powered weekly roadmaps for students who know they're in the wrong place but don't know how to get out.

> The hardest design problem: how do you ask a 19-year-old who they are without making them feel like they're filling out a form?

**[notes.ai](https://notes-ai-dev.lovable.app/)** — _no more last-night stress._

I read YouTube comments for a month before building this. The pattern was everywhere: engineering students, night before exam, 4-hour playlist, no time. notes.ai converts any lecture URL into chapter-organized notes and revision sheets. The problem wasn't information — it was time.

**[Sparr](https://sparr.in/)** — _a partner in thinking._

Submit a hypothesis. Sparr finds the weak assumption and attacks it specifically. Not generically. The prompt engineering problem here is harder than it looks — the difference between "here's a counterargument" and "here's exactly where your reasoning breaks" is significant. Still improving it.

**[Velocity](https://ursvelocity.lovable.app/)** — _businesses for small creators, automated._

Creators know how to make content. Most don't know how to build a business. Velocity analyzes audience pain points and turns them into validated product ideas, pricing strategy, and launch plans.

**[LocalBiz Copilot](https://localbiz-copilot.lovable.app/)** — _local businesses, optimised for growth._

Building the other products taught me something: AI is a better long-term decision-maker than instinct, because today's decision compounds into tomorrow's outcome — and most people, especially small business owners, only have time to think about today.

LocalBiz Copilot makes that kind of decision-making accessible to people who'd never call themselves "data-driven." Simple UI, built for someone running a shop. Underneath, it's reasoning through data and logic to make probabilistic calls — and it explains what each decision actually means for the business down the line, not just what to do right now.

**[thedrishtiverma.com](https://thedrishtiverma.com)** — _Actively building!_

The most personal thing I'm working on. If UNTRAP is for other people figuring out who they are, this is me doing the same thing — except I'm building the mirror myself. More in Current Obsession, below.

## 02 · How I Think

It starts with noticing something. A problem someone's living with that they've stopped noticing themselves.

Then a layer most people skip — I sit with it. Reasoning, perspective, past experience, reading around it until the problem has a real shape. The question I keep coming back to:

> If I were in their place, what would actually help me?

I think in words. A long quiet conversation with myself — opinion, counter-opinion, revision. 

Then I sketch. Not the solution yet — the problem, mostly. Sketching is how I join the layers of my own thinking into something I can actually see. 

After that, I build. I use AI for research, for prototyping, for rapid iteration toward something close to what I imagined. I say that plainly because it's true — I can't do everything alone, and I don't need to pretend I can. **AI is my leverage**. When I get stuck, I don't grind through it. I switch the angle. Ask the question differently. Let AI push back on the gap I'm avoiding instead of staying stuck in it.

I'm not fast. I'm deep. I used to think that would cost me something eventually. It never has — going deep is what gets me into flow, and flow is where the actual work happens. I run to-do lists every day, keep things organized, and usually finish before I have to. A few of my friends picked up the same habit just from watching how I work. 

**The thinking is the work. Everything after is just execution.**

## 03 · Principles

**Observe before you build.**
Every project I've shipped started with something I personally saw. Not a market gap. Not a trend. A real person with a real problem I couldn't stop noticing. If I can't point to the observation, I don't start.

**Simplicity is a decision you make at the start, not a polish you add at the end.**
Early versions of everything I've built had too many features. I kept adding because removing felt like admitting I was wrong. Removing is the harder skill. I'm still learning it.

**AI is leverage, not identity.**
I use it openly. It makes me faster, sharper, and more honest about my gaps. But the observation, the problem definition, the product decision — that's mine. Tools don't think. I do.

**Own the failure before someone else names it.**
When something doesn't work, the answer is rarely the user's fault. It's almost always that I didn't understand the problem well enough yet.

**Flow over force.**
I don't grind. I organize, I prepare, I get into flow — and in flow I can do more in two hours than most people do in a day. Grinding is what happens when you skip the thinking.

**I don't optimize for jobs. I optimize for building and selling.**
The difference compounds.

## 04 · The Inner Work - Phase After NDA

I've been journalling for six years. Whatever comes out — problems I'm stuck on, how I'm feeling that moment, ideas half-formed, thinking I haven't finished yet.

Nobody reads it. That's the point.

When the NDA rejection hit, I stopped for a while. Buried myself in distractions instead. And what I noticed, once I came back to it, was that the distractions had made everything louder — because I'd stopped processing anything. The journal didn't fix the rejection. It just gave me back access to myself. I went through old entries. Read who I was before the thing I didn't get. Got clarity on something simple but not obvious at the time:
I was focused on what I could have become if I'd passed. The journal reminded me who I already was.

That shift — from external outcome to internal identity — is the most useful thing I've ever learned. And I learned it from reading my own words back to myself.

The other thing six years of writing to yourself does: it makes you better at understanding other people. Not because you become more empathetic in an abstract way. Because you've spent years noticing your own patterns — how you reason, how you feel, how you get stuck — and that noticing transfers. You start recognising the same patterns in other people. When someone is stuck, you understand what kind of stuck it is. When someone is hurting, you don't react. You actually listen. 

You remain patient and calm, which life hits harder.

People around me started noticing, the conversations got more meaningful. The advice landed differently. I didn't have a framework for it — I was just being helpful the way I've always been by nature. But I think the journalling is what made it precise.

It's also, I've realised, why I build what I build. 

UNTRAP came from watching people who didn't know themselves well enough to choose their own path. 

Even notes.ai is about giving people back something they already have — time, clarity, confidence.

*The software is downstream of the inner work. Always has been.*

## 06 · Experiments

Things I'm actively testing and haven't resolved:

- **Can a RAG system trained only on personal artifacts represent someone authentically — or does it inevitably flatten them?** Building _d-idea_ to find out.
- **How specific can a prompt get before it stops generalizing?** Sparr's counterargument quality depends on this. Currently exploring multi-stage prompting to separate hypothesis parsing from argument generation.
- **What's the minimum viable self-discovery flow?** UNTRAP's assessment is too long. Cutting it without losing signal is the current design problem.
- **Is Supabase the right long-term backend for UNTRAP?** It's working now. I'm not sure it scales the way I want. Watching the edges.

## 07 · Engineering Decisions (and Why)

*The things I chose and why — not because they're right, because they're what I know.*

**Why Supabase over Firebase:** Auth, real-time, and PostgreSQL in one. Firebase scared me with its pricing at scale. Supabase felt more honest about what it was.

**Why Gemini APIs over OpenAI:** Cost at the prototype stage, and multimodal capability I haven't fully used yet but want to. Will probably use both as needs diverge.

**Why RAG for _d-idea_:** For Mmst LLM's, RAG is the only architecture where I can say "this response came from something I wrote" and mean it.

**Why Lovable for prototyping:** Speed to first version. I'm honest about this — Lovable generated most of the scaffolding. My contribution is the product decisions, prompt architecture, and API integration. That's a real skillset. It's not the same as systems engineering. I'm working on closing that gap.
